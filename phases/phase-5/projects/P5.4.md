# P5.4 — `breakpoint`

> Load-test driftwatch until it breaks, find out why, fix it, and show the graph.

**Phase 5** · ships week 32 · k6 + GitHub Actions · difficulty Medium
**Spec:** [`plan/01-roadmap.md`](../../../plan/01-roadmap.md) → search `P5.4`

## Why this one

This is the payoff for [`P4.3`](../../phase-4/projects/P4.3.md). A load test without traces
tells you *that* the service broke; with traces it tells you *where*, which is the
difference between a graph and a diagnosis. That reordering — observability before load
testing — is a deliberate change in [`plan/01-roadmap.md`](../../../plan/01-roadmap.md), and
this is where it pays. It ships against `driftwatch` itself rather than a toy target, so
every number is about a service you wrote, deployed and can instrument. It comes after
[`P5.2`](P5.2.md) because there is now an asynchronous path worth breaking: producer, queue
and consumer give way in different places and at different loads, and finding out which goes
first is the interesting part.

## What you'll be able to do after

- You will have written a k6 suite yourself — smoke, load, stress, spike — and can explain
  what each shape is for and why a single "load test" answers almost nothing.
- You will be able to state your own breaking point as a rate and name the resource that
  caused it, instead of saying the service is fast.
- You will have found a bottleneck from a trace, fixed exactly one thing and measured the
  effect, so you can defend the fix rather than assert it.
- You will have a smoke load test in CI on every deploy and can explain what threshold it
  guards.

bottleneck, and the one-line fix with its measured effect. Week 33's buffer exists to
publish the benchmark write-up; this is the project that gives you something worth
publishing.

## Where you'll get stuck

- **You measure the load generator, not the service** — throughput flattens while the
  service's own metrics look bored, because the generator saturated first or is opening a
  new connection per request. → check generator CPU and connection reuse before believing
  any plateau; every conclusion drawn from a saturated generator is wrong.
- **The first bottleneck is not your code** — it is the connection pool. Serverless
  invocations plus a pooled Postgres means concurrency is capped somewhere you did not
  write, and the symptom is climbing latency while database CPU stays low. → read the
  pooler's own documentation and its metrics, not a general article about connection limits.
  The 2-hour rule fires when a run stops being reproducible: write "I expect X, I observe
  Y", shrink the scenario, then read the source.
- **You fix two things at once** — the second graph looks better and you cannot say which
  change did it, which destroys the only interesting claim in the repo. → one change per
  run, and keep the losing runs in the results directory.
- **Heavy runs eat metered budget in an afternoon** — a stress test spends compute and
  request allowance far faster than normal use. Ceilings are in
  [`../verified/`](../verified/), and the discipline is: ramp against local Docker Postgres,
  use the deployed stack only for the final validated run, and say so in the README. That is
  the professional answer, not an apology.

## The interview angle

"It handles a known rate before the connection limit bites, and here is the graph" is a
categorically better answer than "it's fast", and few candidates can give it. The rep here
is capacity reasoning: what saturates first, why tail latency turns before throughput does,
and what a CI threshold actually protects. Resume line: *load-tested my own service to its
breaking point, diagnosed the bottleneck from traces, fixed it and measured the
improvement.* The follow-up is "what breaks next?" — and the honest answer is the second
bottleneck you found and left alone, because you had one week and took the biggest.

## Scope discipline

**Out of scope** — no distributed load generation (infrastructure work that teaches nothing
new here); no chaos engineering (a separate discipline and a separate project); no
cost-per-request modelling, which belongs to [`P6.7`](../../phase-6/projects/P6.7.md).

**Stretch** — a k6 test that specifically proves [`P5.2`](P5.2.md)'s idempotency guarantee
under concurrency: duplicate deliveries at load, exactly one record. Only after the ship
checklist is green.
